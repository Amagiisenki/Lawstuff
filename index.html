What we found

From the court’s desk

Access can look like available services, orderly proceedings, timely notices, and efficient case administration.

From the visitor’s side

Access means being able to find the right place, understand what is happening, and take part in decisions that affect you.

Open each finding to see what it means in practice.

Finding the court and getting inside

The Makati MeTC, a first-level court, appeared clean and welcoming. Accommodating staff, attention to language at the entrance, culturally responsive spaces, and amenities such as elevators made the building feel more approachable.

Visitors could still struggle to navigate between buildings or use information boards to locate a courtroom, schedule, or procedure without asking staff. Long elevator lines and parking constraints could create particular difficulties for seniors and persons with disabilities.

Put it to work. Give someone the building, branch, floor, and room. Ask whether the route and waiting arrangements work for them and identify whom they can approach on arrival.
Waiting, scheduling, and courtroom coordination

At the observed Makati RTC session, proceedings scheduled for 8:30 a.m. began around 10 a.m. after the fiscal arrived late. Of the 11 criminal cases calendared, five involved B.P. 22. Limited seating meant staff repeatedly called parties waiting outside. A late motion and an unavailable witness showed how preparation across different roles affects a hearing.

Put it to work. Explain how the person will know when their case is called. Consider how waiting or a postponement affects transport, work, and availability.
Having counsel and understanding what a plea means

During the observed RTC arraignment, an accused without private counsel was assisted by a PAO lawyer appointed by the court. Their first conversation took place during the proceedings, when counsel had to guide the accused on entering a plea. The court offered language options and conducted the arraignment in Filipino, but legal terms and conventions still required explanation.

Put it to work. Arrange time for the client to speak privately with counsel. Explain the purpose of the hearing, what the person may be asked, and what technical terms mean. Invite them to explain the next step in their own words.
Frontline staff as a link between the public and the court

In the MeTC Branch 65 interview, Clerk of Court Atty. Paul Jeffrey Urag Toguay described staff listening to complaints, following up on pending cases and motions, reminding judges about overdue decisions, and maintaining accurate hearing records and notices.

His account also emphasized calm responses to frustrated visitors, acknowledgment of their time and stress, and help connecting people with representation through PAO for qualified indigent litigants or counsel de officio when needed and available.

Put it to work. Acknowledge the concern first. Give one concrete next step and identify who can help the person follow through.
Electronic processes, hybrid hearings, and the digital divide

Electronic filing and email can reduce paper handling and speed document transmission. Videoconferencing can ease travel and scheduling burdens. The RTC session was generally in person, with one case heard by videoconference.

Devices, data, reliable internet, and digital literacy affect whether people can use these services. The MeTC interview described guiding parties through requirements and considering a physical hearing when videoconferencing access fails.

Put it to work. Ask how the person will open a notice or join a hearing. Confirm a contact and the applicable next step if the technology fails.
Institutional constraints and the SPJI

The interview described cases dating to the 1980s with records still to be digitized, roughly 300 active cases in the interviewee’s branch, and administrative breakdowns involving missed hearing entries, notices, and postponements.

The presentation connects the Strategic Plan for Judicial Innovations (SPJI) priorities of efficiency, innovation, and access to electronic filing, videoconferencing, and high-performance courts. A faster process can still shift costs and work to people who need devices, connectivity, or technical help.

Put it to work. When assessing a reform, ask both what becomes easier for the court and what a person must now do to exercise their rights. For example, consider whether parties receive and understand notices in time to act alongside how quickly the court issues them.
